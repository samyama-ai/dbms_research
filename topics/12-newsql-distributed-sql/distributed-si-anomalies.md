# Distributed snapshot isolation anomaly characterization

> **Topic:** NewSQL & Distributed SQL · **ID:** `12-newsql-distributed-sql/distributed-si-anomalies` · **Status:** partially-solved

## 1. Problem Statement

Snapshot isolation (SI) permits non-serializable histories whose canonical
representative is **write skew**: two transactions read an overlapping snapshot, each
writes a disjoint item, and the conjunction of their writes violates an invariant no
serial order would. The problem is to give a **complete, decidable characterization** of
the anomalies admitted by *distributed* and *partitioned* SI — variants in which the
snapshot is assembled across shards with independent clocks/watermarks, commit timestamps
are only partially ordered, and the "first-committer-wins" (FCW) check is enforced
per-partition rather than globally.

Concretely: (i) **Decision variant** — given a history $H$ over a partitioning $P$ and a
distributed-SI implementation $\mathcal{I}$, decide whether $H$ is admissible under
$\mathcal{I}$ and whether it is serializable. (ii) **Characterization variant** —
enumerate the structural conditions on the *dependency graph* that distinguish the
anomalies reachable under distributed SI (parallel SI, generalized SI, clock-SI,
per-shard FCW) from those reachable under classical single-version SI. (iii)
**Static-analysis variant** — given an application's transaction templates, decide
whether *any* distributed-SI execution can violate a declared integrity constraint.

## 2. Mathematical Foundations

The standard formalism is Adya's **dependency-graph (DSG)** characterization. For a
history, build a graph on committed transactions with edges $ww$ (version order), $wr$
(read-from), and $rw$ (anti-dependency / "rw-conflict"). Adya's theorem: SI corresponds
to forbidding a specific structure, and Fekete et al. proved that **every non-serializable
SI history contains a cycle with at least two consecutive $rw$ anti-dependency edges**,
both incident on "pivot" transactions. Formally, serializability failure under SI implies
a cycle $\dots \xrightarrow{rw} T_{piv} \xrightarrow{rw} \dots$ in the DSG.

Distribution changes the version-order and timestamp model. Let each transaction carry a
commit timestamp $c(T)$ from a partial order $\preceq$ rather than a total order; under
Clock-SI the snapshot time $s(T)$ and $c(T)$ derive from loosely synchronized clocks with
uncertainty $\epsilon$. Generalized SI (GSI) relaxes the snapshot to *any* causally
consistent past cut, enlarging the admissible set. The characterization question is which
**multi-version serialization graph (MVSG)** cycles become realizable when $\preceq$ is a
partial order and FCW is checked over a sharded version space.

## 3. State of the Art (SOTA)

**Theory SOTA.** Fekete–Liarokapis–O'Neil–O'Neil–Shasha (TODS 2005) gave the dangerous-
structure theorem and a static-analysis method for making applications "serializable on
SI" by introducing conflicts. Cerone–Bernardi–Gotsman (CONCUR 2015) gave an
**axiomatic, framework** specifying SI, parallel SI, and related models via visibility/
arbitration relations, and characterized their anomalies uniformly. Cerone–Gotsman
(PODC 2016 / TODS) extended this to robustness criteria.

**Systems SOTA.** Clock-SI (Du–Elnikety–Zwaenepoel, SRDS 2013) implements distributed SI
without a global timestamp authority; CockroachDB, YugabyteDB, and TiDB enforce
serializable or SI-like isolation with per-key/per-range conflict checks. Robustness
tooling (detecting whether a fixed workload is SI-robust, i.e., always serializable) was
advanced by Beillahi–Bouajjani–Enea (POPL 2019, ESOP 2020).

## 4. Upper Bound

For a *fixed finite* history, checking SI-admissibility and serializability is solvable by
DSG construction in polynomial time in the history size. **SI-robustness** of a set of
transaction templates against a parametric workload is decidable for several models:
Beillahi et al. reduce robustness against SI to reachability and give a **PSPACE**
algorithm for parameterized programs, and polynomial procedures for restricted template
classes via the absence of dangerous (two-$rw$-edge) cycles. The Cerone–Gotsman criterion
yields a static check over the *static dependency graph* of templates in time polynomial
in the number of templates for SI.

## 5. Lower Bound

General robustness/serializability decision against weak isolation is hard: checking
serializability of an arbitrary history is **NP-complete** (Papadimitriou 1979) for the
view-serializability variant; conflict-serializability of a fixed history is polynomial,
but robustness *against all workloads* generated by templates is **PSPACE-hard**
(Beillahi–Bouajjani–Enea). For distributed/causal variants, the added partial-order
arbitration makes the admissible-history membership problem at least as hard as the
single-site case, and certain GSI robustness questions are undecidable for
Turing-powerful transaction programs (reductions from reachability in counter machines).

## 6. The Gap

A *uniform* anomaly characterization exists for the established models (SI, PSI, GSI) via
the Cerone–Gotsman/Adya frameworks, so the problem is partially solved. What remains open:
(1) a **tight, model-by-model taxonomy** of which write-skew-class anomalies survive under
each concrete distributed implementation (Clock-SI with bounded $\epsilon$, per-shard FCW,
HLC-based commit) versus are excluded; (2) closing the decidability/complexity gap between
the PSPACE robustness bound and known hardness for *interactive* multi-round transactions;
(3) extending characterization to **dynamic** read/write sets and predicate reads
(phantoms), where the DSG must account for gap locks / range anti-dependencies.

## 7. Current Research (as of June 2026)

Active threads: axiomatic specification and robustness verification (Gotsman, Enea,
Bouajjani groups); automated SI-robustness checkers integrated into CI for CockroachDB-
and Postgres-class workloads *(frontier — verify)*; and formal-methods work mechanizing
the dangerous-structure theorem in Coq/Isabelle for distributed commit protocols. There is
growing interest in characterizing anomalies under **HLC + bounded-staleness follower
reads**, where the snapshot cut is intentionally stale *(frontier — verify)*.

## 8. Future Work

- A decidable, implementation-parametric calculus mapping a concrete distributed-SI design
  to its exact admissible-anomaly set.
- Phantom/predicate-aware DSG characterization for SQL range scans under distributed SI.
- Quantitative robustness: bounding the *rate* of anomalies, not just their possibility,
  given a contention/skew model.
- Synthesis: automatically inserting the minimum conflict-promoting writes (or
  `SELECT ... FOR UPDATE`) to make a workload SI-robust at least cost.

## 10. Worked Example

Classic write skew. Invariant: at least one of two on-call doctors must stay (`x + y \ge 1`), with $x=y=1$ initially. The rows sit on **different shards** $S_x, S_y$.

- $T_1$ reads snapshot $\{x{=}1, y{=}1\}$, sees $y=1$, sets $x \leftarrow 0$ (writes only on $S_x$).
- $T_2$ reads the same snapshot, sees $x=1$, sets $y \leftarrow 0$ (writes only on $S_y$).

Per-shard first-committer-wins finds no conflict: $T_1$ touches only $x$, $T_2$ only $y$, so each commits. Result $x=y=0$ — invariant violated, yet no serial order ($T_1;T_2$ or $T_2;T_1$) produces it.

DSG check: $T_1 \xrightarrow{rw} T_2$ (T_1 read $y$, T_2 overwrote it) and $T_2 \xrightarrow{rw} T_1$ (T_2 read $x$, T_1 overwrote it). This is a cycle with two consecutive $rw$ edges — exactly Fekete et al.'s dangerous structure, both transactions acting as pivots. A global FCW or SSI promotion would abort one; the sharded check cannot see the cross-shard cycle.
